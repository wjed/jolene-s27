# How these decisions actually get made

Useful context for where to spend effort. None of this is a secret. It is just
not written down anywhere an applicant naturally looks.

---

## Who reads the application

For a large program (CHOP, St. Jude), it is usually two stages:

1. **A screen** against hard criteria: eligibility, citizenship, class year, GPA
   floor, completeness. This stage is mechanical. Most rejections happen here and
   have nothing to do with how good the applicant is.
2. **Faculty review.** Files get routed to labs by research area. A PI scans for:
   does this person want to do what we do, and can they be useful for ten weeks?

The second stage is the one worth optimizing for, and it hinges on specificity.

NIH SIP works differently. There is no central committee: individual NIH
investigators read the applicant pool and choose their own interns, starting in
early January. Your cover letter and your emails to PIs are the whole
application there, which is why the
[cold email](../templates/cold-email-pi.md) matters most at NIH.

## What the screen is checking

Failing any of these wastes the whole application:

- **What you plan to do next.** Amgen and the Broad ask for an interest in a PhD
  or MD-PhD, and Penn State SURIP says it is not meant for students who plan to
  apply to medical school. If you are aiming at medicine only, these screen you
  out as surely as a GPA floor.
- **Research experience already done.** St. Jude POE requires research
  experience outside the classroom when you apply, and your lab job should
  count. NYU Langone SURP wants at least one full semester of bench lab
  research, which a psychology lab may not satisfy, so check with them before
  you spend the hours.
- **GPA floor.** St. Jude wants 3.40 in science and math and 3.40 overall. UPMC
  Children's Pittsburgh wants 3.5, CHOP 3.3, Amgen and the Broad 3.2, and Mayo,
  Vanderbilt and the Leadership Alliance 3.0. These are hard filters.
- **Class year.** Fred Hutch takes only students entering the summer before
  their final year, which is you. Many programs exclude students who graduate
  before or during the summer, and Seattle Children's excludes anyone
  graduating before or during the program.
- **Availability for the full term.** St. Jude requires one of four specific
  start dates (10, 17 or 24 May, or 1 June) and eleven weeks from
  undergraduates. The Broad requires all nine weeks. If you have a conflict,
  address it before applying, not after.
- **Completeness.** Letters that arrive after the deadline sink otherwise strong
  applications. St. Jude's deadline covers every credential, and Penn SUIP's
  letters must arrive by the application deadline itself.
- **Citizenship and residency.** NSF REUs want a US citizen, US national or
  permanent resident, and NIH SIP, Amgen and many others ask for much the same.
  As a US citizen you clear all of these. Residency is the exception: NYSCF
  takes only New York City residents, which is why it is off your list.

The site's filters exist mostly to stop you spending four hours on a program you
are not eligible for. Fill in My eligibility and the site flags every program
that conflicts, with the reason.

## What the faculty review is weighing

Roughly in order:

1. **Fit with a specific lab.** The single biggest factor, and the one most
   within your control. An application that names a PI, references their work, and
   says why, lands in a different pile. If you have emailed that PI and they said
   yes, it is a different pile again.
2. **Evidence of follow-through.** Ten weeks is short. They are looking for signs
   someone will not need babysitting: a project you ran, a protocol you handled,
   a semester of consistent lab hours, a job that required showing up.
3. **A real reason for the area.** Not passion, a question. "I want to understand
   why seizure burden predicts developmental outcome" tells a reader where to
   route the file.
4. **Letters.** Mostly used as corroboration. A detailed letter from someone who
   supervised you beats a glowing one from someone who taught you in a lecture.
5. **Grades and coursework.** Matter at the screen, matter less here, as long as
   the relevant courses are there.

## What does not move the needle

- Enthusiasm as such. Everyone is enthusiastic.
- Volume of extracurriculars unrelated to research or clinical work.
- Prestige-signaling about the program in the essay. They know it is good.
- A longer personal statement. Over-length is a mild negative.

## The four asymmetries worth exploiting

**One application, many labs.** NIH SIP, the Leadership Alliance and
Vanderbilt's VSSA each put a single application in front of many labs or
institutions. Investigators all over NIH can read your SIP application, the
Leadership Alliance application ranks three of its 20 sites, and VSSA runs many
tracks through one portal. The marginal cost of the second, third and fifth lab
is small. These should be done first and done well.

Amgen Scholars is the near miss. There is no single Amgen application: each of
the 14 US hosts runs its own, with its own portal and its own letter request to
your recommenders. The essays are reusable and the paperwork is not, so count
each extra host as half an application rather than a free one, and tell your
recommenders how many there will be.

**Cold emails.** Fifteen minutes, and the only thing that converts an anonymous
file into a named candidate. Most applicants never send one. At NIH they are
part of the application. [Template](../templates/cold-email-pi.md).

**Less-famous places with real research.** Arkansas Children's, Stowers, Van
Andel, UAB, Einstein, Nemours and Benaroya do genuine work and receive a fraction
of the applications that CHOP and St. Jude get. A summer with a real project and
a mentor who had time beats a rejection from a famous program. Build the list
with a few of these in it deliberately.

**Living at home.** A program you can commute to is worth more than its stipend
says, because none of it goes on rent. NIH, Janelia, Inova, Children's National
and Georgetown are all within reach of Leesburg, and so, at a stretch, are
Hopkins and Kennedy Krieger in Baltimore. See the
[Leesburg rule](../guides/reading-a-program-page.md).

## On the thin-experience problem

If your research experience is light, that is normal going into your senior-year
summer, and several programs are built for it. Seattle Children's says on its
own page that prior formal research experience is not required and that
applicants seeking their first formal research internship are prioritized. NSF
REUs are designed as entry points.

Two on this list go the other way, and it is a mechanical screen rather than a
judgment call. St. Jude POE requires research experience outside the classroom
at the time you apply. NYU Langone SURP wants at least one full semester of
bench lab research. Check those two before you spend the hours.

What helps: a serious course project described as research, specific technical
skills you can actually perform, and a letter from someone who has watched you
work. What does not help: apologizing for it in the personal statement.

## A realistic expectation

These programs run acceptance rates in the low single digits to low teens. Strong
applicants get rejected from most places they apply. The right response is volume
on the reusable applications plus a handful of genuinely attainable targets, not
six perfect applications to six famous programs.

Twelve to twenty applications is a reasonable target for this cycle, and the
structure in this repo is built to make that a realistic amount of work rather
than an impossible one.
