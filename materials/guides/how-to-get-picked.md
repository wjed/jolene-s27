# How these decisions actually get made

Useful context for where to spend effort. None of this is a secret. It is just
not written down anywhere an applicant naturally looks.

---

## Who reads the application

For a large program (NIH SIP, CHOP, St. Jude), it is usually two stages:

1. **A screen** against hard criteria: eligibility, citizenship, class year, GPA
   floor, completeness. This stage is mechanical. Most rejections happen here and
   have nothing to do with how good the applicant is.
2. **Faculty review.** Files get routed to labs by research area. A PI scans for:
   does this person want to do what we do, and can they be useful for ten weeks?

The second stage is the one worth optimising for, and it hinges on specificity.

## What the screen is checking

Every one of these is in the dataset, and failing any of them wastes the whole
application:

- **Citizenship.** NSF REUs, NIH SIP, Amgen and many others require US citizen or
  permanent resident. No exceptions, no appeals. Filter on this first.
- **Class year.** Fred Hutch takes rising seniors *only*. Many programs exclude
  students who graduate before or during the summer. Seattle Children's excludes
  anyone graduating during the program.
- **GPA floor.** St. Jude wants 3.40 in math/science and overall. Amgen wants 3.2.
  These are hard filters.
- **Availability for the full term.** St. Jude requires one of four specific start
  dates and rejects applicants who cannot make one. The Broad requires all nine
  weeks. If you have a conflict, address it before applying, not after.
- **Completeness.** Letters that arrive after the deadline sink otherwise strong
  applications. St. Jude's deadline covers credentials too.

The site's filters exist mostly to stop you spending four hours on a program you
are not eligible for.

## What the faculty review is weighing

Roughly in order:

1. **Fit with a specific lab.** The single biggest factor, and the one most
   within your control. An application that names a PI, references their work, and
   says why, lands in a different pile. If you have emailed that PI and they said
   yes, it is a different pile again.
2. **Evidence of follow-through.** Ten weeks is short. They are looking for signs
   someone will not need babysitting: a project you ran, a protocol you handled,
   a semester of consistent lab hours, a job that required showing up.
3. **A real reason for the area.** Not passion, a question. "I want to understand
   why seizure burden predicts developmental outcome" tells a reader where to
   route the file.
4. **Letters.** Mostly used as corroboration. A detailed letter from someone who
   supervised you beats a glowing one from someone who taught you in a lecture.
5. **Grades and coursework.** Matter at the screen, matter less here, as long as
   the relevant courses are there.

## What does not move the needle

- Enthusiasm as such. Everyone is enthusiastic.
- Volume of extracurriculars unrelated to research or clinical work.
- Prestige-signalling about the program in the essay. They know it is good.
- A longer personal statement. Over-length is a mild negative.

## The three asymmetries worth exploiting

**One application, many labs.** NIH SIP, Amgen Scholars, the Leadership Alliance
and Vanderbilt's VSSA each put a single essay in front of many institutions or
many labs. The marginal cost of the second, third and fifth is small. These
should be done first and done well.

**Cold emails.** Fifteen minutes, and the only thing that converts an anonymous
file into a named candidate. Most applicants never send one.
[Template](../templates/cold-email-pi.md).

**Less-famous places with real research.** Arkansas Children's, Stowers, Van
Andel, UAB, Einstein, Nemours and Benaroya do genuine work and receive a fraction
of the applications that CHOP and St. Jude get. A summer with a real project and
a mentor who had time beats a rejection from a famous program. Build the list
with a few of these in it deliberately.

## On the thin-experience problem

If your research experience is light, that is the normal state for a rising junior
and several programs are built for it, Seattle Children's explicitly prioritises
first-time research interns, and NSF REUs are designed as entry points.

What helps: a serious course project described as research, specific technical
skills you can actually perform, and a letter from someone who has watched you
work. What does not help: apologising for it in the personal statement.

## A realistic expectation

These programs run acceptance rates in the low single digits to low teens. Strong
applicants get rejected from most places they apply. The right response is volume
on the reusable applications plus a handful of genuinely attainable targets, not
six perfect applications to six famous programs.

Twelve to twenty applications is a reasonable target for this cycle, and the
structure in this repo is built to make that a realistic amount of work rather
than an impossible one.
