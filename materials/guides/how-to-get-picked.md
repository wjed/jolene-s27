# How these decisions actually get made

Useful context for where to spend your effort. None of this is a secret. It is
just not written down anywhere an applicant naturally looks. Your own strengths
and gaps come near the end.

---

## Who reads the application

For a program with its own selection committee (CHOP, St. Jude), it is usually
two stages:

1. **A screen** against hard criteria: eligibility rules such as citizenship and
   class year, and completeness. This stage is mechanical. Most rejections
   happen here and have nothing to do with how good the applicant is.
2. **Faculty review.** Files get routed to labs by research area. A PI scans for:
   does this person want to do what we do, and can they be useful for ten weeks?

The second stage is the one worth optimizing for, and it hinges on specificity.
Your record already answers the second question better than most. The work goes
into the first, answered separately for each lab.

NIH SIP works differently. There is no central committee: individual NIH
investigators read the applicant pool and choose their own interns, starting in
early January. Your cover letter and your emails to PIs do most of the work
there, which is why the [cold email](../templates/cold-email-pi.md) matters most
at NIH. NIH is also a commute from home, and NICHD (child health), NIMH (mood
and anxiety) and NINDS (neurology) between them cover what you want to study.

## What the screen is checking

Failing any of these wastes the whole application:

- **What you plan to do next.** Amgen and the Broad ask for an interest in a PhD
  or MD-PhD, Penn SUIP and the Leadership Alliance are built for students
  heading to a PhD, and Penn State SURIP says it is not meant for students who
  plan to apply to medical school. You are premed and still deciding between an
  MD and an MD-PhD, so this is the screen to think hardest about. What to do
  about it is under your gaps, below.
- **Research experience already done.** St. Jude POE requires research
  experience outside the classroom when you apply, and your lab work is exactly
  that. NYU Langone SURP wants at least one full semester of bench lab research.
  Yours is with people, not at a bench, so that one is out this cycle.
- **Class year.** Never a problem for you. Every program on the site takes
  juniors, and Fred Hutch takes only students entering the summer before their
  final year, which is you.
- **Availability for the full term.** St. Jude requires one of four start dates
  (10, 17 or 24 May, or 1 June) and eleven weeks from undergraduates. The Broad
  requires all nine weeks, with no other courses or job. If anything else claims
  part of your summer, such as an MCAT date, raise it before applying, not after.
- **Completeness.** Letters that arrive after the deadline sink otherwise strong
  applications. Penn SUIP's letters, for one, must arrive by the application
  deadline itself.
- **Citizenship and residency.** As a US citizen you clear every citizenship
  rule on this list. Residency is the exception: NYSCF takes only New York City
  residents, which is why it is off your list.

The site's filters exist mostly to stop you spending four hours on a program you
are not eligible for. Fill in My eligibility and the site flags every program
that conflicts, with the reason.

## What the faculty review is weighing

Roughly in order:

1. **Fit with a specific lab.** The single biggest factor, and the one most
   within your control. An application that names a PI, references their work,
   and says why, lands in a different pile. If you have emailed that PI and they
   said yes, it is a different pile again.
2. **Evidence of follow-through.** Ten weeks is short. They are looking for signs
   someone will not need babysitting: a project you ran, a protocol you handled,
   a semester of consistent lab hours, a job that required showing up. This is
   your strongest ground (see what you bring, below).
3. **A real reason for the area.** Not passion, a question, specific enough to
   tell a reader where to route the file. One could grow straight out of your
   lab: how mood and anxiety disorders show up in children, and whether emotion
   tracking like your lab's could pick them up. Write yours in one sentence:
   [your question].
4. **Letters.** Mostly used as corroboration. A detailed letter from someone who
   supervised you beats a glowing one from someone who taught you in a lecture.
   For you, that is whoever supervises your lab work, then a professor who has
   watched you up close, such as the one whose course you TA.
5. **Coursework.** It matters less here than at the screen, as long as the
   relevant courses are there, and yours are: general and organic chemistry,
   biochemistry, physics, calculus and psychology.

## What does not move the needle

- Enthusiasm as such. Everyone is enthusiastic.
- Volume of extracurriculars unrelated to research or clinical work.
- Prestige-signaling about the program in the essay. They know it is good.
- A longer personal statement. Over-length is a mild negative.
- A long case for why you want to be a doctor. Here your clinical work is
  evidence that you are reliable, not the subject: a research program is
  choosing a researcher.

## The four asymmetries worth exploiting

**One application, many labs.** NIH SIP and Vanderbilt's VSSA each put a single
application in front of many labs. Investigators all over NIH can read your SIP
application, and VSSA runs many tracks through one portal. The marginal cost of
the second, third and fifth lab is small, so do these first and do them well.
The Leadership Alliance works the same way, ranking three of its 20 sites from
one application, but it says it is not designed for clinical medicine careers,
so it belongs on your list only if an MD-PhD becomes a firm plan.

Amgen Scholars is the near miss. There is no single Amgen application: each of
the 14 US hosts runs its own, with its own portal and its own letter request to
your recommenders. The essays are reusable and the paperwork is not, so count
each extra host as half an application rather than a free one, and tell your
recommenders how many there will be.

**Cold emails.** Fifteen minutes, and the only thing that converts an anonymous
file into a named candidate. Most applicants never send one. At NIH they are
part of the application, and near home they are often the only way in: Inova
and Georgetown post pages about research and training, not summer applications
with deadlines. [Template](../templates/cold-email-pi.md).

**Less-famous places with real research.** Arkansas Children's, Stowers, Van
Andel, UAB, Einstein, Nemours and Benaroya do genuine work and receive a fraction
of the applications that CHOP and St. Jude get. A summer with a real project and
a mentor who had time beats a rejection from a famous program. Build the list
with a few of these in it deliberately. Arkansas Children's and Nemours are
pediatric. The rest lean toward basic science, so read the For you note on each
before you commit the hours.

**Living at home.** A program you can commute to is worth more than its stipend
says, because none of it goes on rent. From home you can reach NIH in Bethesda,
Janelia in Ashburn, Inova in Fairfax, and Children's National, Georgetown,
George Washington and Howard in Washington. Baltimore, for Johns Hopkins,
Kennedy Krieger and the University of Maryland, is about 70 minutes each way:
possible, but a long commute. The Can live at home filter in All Programs lists
them all. See the [living-at-home rule](../guides/reading-a-program-page.md).

## Your strengths and your gaps

### What you bring

- **More than a year of human-subjects research.** Since August 2025 you have
  run participant sessions, coded data, worked on technology-based emotion
  tracking and troubleshot study protocols. Describe it in verbs, and add the
  one problem you solved: [a protocol problem, and what you changed].
- **Three CITI certificates.** Most undergraduates have none. Put them near the
  top of your CV, where a human-subjects lab will see them first.
- **Far more patient contact than most applicants.** More than 100 emergency
  calls as a volunteer EMT, about 12 hours a week on top of classes, and a
  summer as a medical assistant seeing up to 26 patients a day. In clinical
  research that transfers directly, and anywhere else it is evidence that you
  show up. Give the numbers every time.
- **Two teaching roles.** Biology Learning Assistant for 80 students, and
  Psychology TA for a 300-student course where you started review sessions
  yourself. That is the initiative a PI hopes for in a summer student.
- **More than five years of shadowing a pediatrician.** This is what makes your
  interest in children believable. Use it to open a question rather than to
  prove commitment: [a moment from shadowing that left you with a question].

### The gaps, and what to do about each

- **No bench experience.** It rules out NYU Langone, and Harvard SHURP asks for
  a semester of college-level lab research, so ask whether a psychology lab
  counts before you spend the hours. Elsewhere the bench is something you would
  learn, not a screen. St. Jude, for one, places undergraduates only in
  laboratory or computational work. Say plainly that it would be new, and what
  carries over: following protocols exactly, coding data carefully, fixing a
  procedure when it breaks. Then aim a good share of your list at research with
  people, where your experience is the job itself, such as CHOP's clinical and
  behavioral tracks. The fit rating on each program weighs exactly this.
- **No poster yet.** Normal before a first summer program, and several of these
  programs end with one: Rochester's Strong Children's Research Center program
  and Einstein both close with a poster session. Meanwhile, ask your PI whether
  any of the lab's work could become a poster this spring with you on it. If an
  abstract is accepted, it goes on your CV that day.
- **Premed, where some programs want future PhDs.** Penn State SURIP is out
  either way, since an MD-PhD is still medical school. For Amgen, the Broad and
  Penn SUIP, decide where an MD-PhD really stands. If it is a real possibility,
  they are a fair test of it, and you can say exactly that: you are deciding,
  and want the summer to help. If you lean toward an MD alone, spend those hours
  on programs open to future physicians, such as Penn's Undergraduate Clinical
  Scholars Program, which says it is built for students aiming at an MD, PhD or
  MD-PhD. Never claim a PhD plan you do not have: an interview will find it out.

What does not help, with any of the three: apologizing in your personal
statement.

## A realistic expectation

These programs run acceptance rates in the low single digits to low teens. Strong
applicants get rejected from most places they apply. The right response is volume
on the reusable applications plus a handful of genuinely attainable targets, not
six perfect applications to six famous programs.

Twelve to twenty applications is a reasonable target for this cycle, and this
site is built to make that a realistic amount of work rather than an impossible
one.
