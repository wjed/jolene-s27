# Reading a program page in five minutes

Program pages bury the things that decide whether an application is worth
starting. This is the order to look for them.

---

## The five disqualifiers. Check these first

Before reading a single word about the research, find:

1. **Citizenship requirement.** "US citizen or permanent resident" appears on
   most federally funded programs. As a US citizen you clear this one, so look
   for residency rules instead: NYSCF takes only New York City residents, which
   is why it is no longer on the site.
2. **Class year.** Look for "rising senior", "must have completed sophomore
   year", "may not graduate before/during the program". In summer 2027 you are
   a rising senior who graduates in May 2028. Every program on the site takes
   that year, and Fred Hutch takes no other, so this check is for programs you
   find elsewhere.
3. **Degree goal.** Some programs exist to send students on to a PhD or an
   MD-PhD. The Broad asks that you be aiming at one, and Penn SUIP and Amgen
   are built for students who are. You have not decided between an MD and an
   MD-PhD. As long as the MD-PhD is a real possibility, undecided is an honest
   place to apply from, so say so plainly. If you settle on the MD alone, the
   Broad is out and the PhD-track programs drop down your list. Penn State
   SURIP is out either way: it says it is not meant for students headed to
   medical school, and an MD-PhD is still medical school.
4. **Dates.** Fixed start dates (St. Jude has four and only four), and whether
   the full term is mandatory. Some programs also rule out any other job or
   class: the Broad and the University of Maryland's Schnaper program (NSIP)
   both do, so a summer at either means no EMT or medical assistant job on the
   side, and no summer course.
5. **Deadline, and what it covers.** Specifically: are letters due on the same
   date, or later? Penn SUIP and the Schnaper program both want letters in by
   the application deadline itself. The Broad gives letters one extra day, and
   Seattle Children's gives them fifteen. Read the hour too: NIH SIP closes at
   noon Eastern on January 26, not at midnight.

If any of these rule you out, stop. That is a win, four hours saved.

## Then find the mentor list

The most valuable page on any program site, and often two clicks deep:
"Faculty", "Mentors", "Research areas", "Labs", or a PDF of last year's projects.

Every program on this site carries a fit rating, and its For you note says how
the program lines up with what you have done and want to study. The mentor list
is where you check that. Search it for your own words: child, adolescent,
development, behavior, anxiety, depression, mood, emotion, neurology. A lab
that studies how mood and anxiety show up in children, or that runs studies
with people, is one where you are useful from the first week. You already run
participant sessions and code data, and you hold three CITI certificates where
most undergraduates have none.

Check, too, what undergraduates actually do there. St. Jude places
undergraduates in laboratory or computational projects and keeps clinical
research for medical students. All of your research so far has been with
people, not at a bench, so at a program like that the bench would be new. Say
so, and point to what carries over: following a protocol exactly, coding data
carefully and troubleshooting a procedure when it fails.

This is where the [why-this-program paragraph](../templates/why-this-program.md)
and the [cold emails](../templates/cold-email-pi.md) come from. A program with no
published mentor list is one where naming a mentor matters less, and a program
with a long list is one where naming a mentor matters a great deal.

## Then the practicalities

- **Stipend**, and whether housing is separate. The two numbers only mean
  something together. Jackson Lab at Bar Harbor pays $7,500 with room, board
  and travel included. Fred Hutch pays $7,304 and leaves housing to you,
  about $3,400 at UW. The stipends are nearly the same, but after rent the
  Fred Hutch one is worth about half as much.
- **Housing**: provided, subsidized, or your problem. "Not provided" is only
  bad news if you have to pay rent, which is what the living-at-home rule
  below is about.
- **Travel**: Jackson Lab and some others cover it; most do not.
- **Length**: usually ten weeks, sometimes eight or nine (St. Jude wants eleven
  from undergraduates), and whether it collides with the end of your spring
  semester at Penn State.

The dataset already has all of this, but verify it on the page before applying.
The stipend figures in particular are the fields most likely to be stale.

## The living-at-home rule

A program you can commute to from home is worth a smaller stipend, because
housing is free at home. With no rent to pay, a stipend with no housing is the
whole stipend. The same offer in Seattle, Boston or New York can lose close to
half of it to rent, as the Fred Hutch numbers above show.

From home in Loudoun County, without traffic:

- **Janelia** in Ashburn: 10 to 20 minutes. Its summer undergraduates come
  through HHMI's Cech Fellows Program, which recruits from partner schools, so
  check whether Penn State is one before anything else.
- **Inova** in Fairfax: 30 to 40 minutes. No housing, and you need none.
- **NIH** in Bethesda: 40 to 50 minutes. NIH SIP provides no housing, which
  does not matter when you are driving in from home.
- **Children's National, Georgetown, George Washington and Howard** in DC:
  45 minutes to an hour. Children's National and GW give no housing, which is
  fine from home.
- **Johns Hopkins, Kennedy Krieger and the University of Maryland** in
  Baltimore: 70 to 80 minutes each way. Here a program with no housing costs
  you something real: about two and a half hours in the car every day, or
  rent. Hopkins Pathway houses its interns free, the Schnaper program offers
  campus housing (ask what it costs), and Kennedy Krieger gives none.

Rush hour adds a lot to all of these, most of all on the drives into DC and
Baltimore, so check each drive at the hour you would actually make it.
The **Can live at home** filter on All Programs lists every program the site
counts as within reach.

So when you compare offers, compare what you keep: the stipend, less rent where
you would pay it, or less gas and parking where you would drive. A smaller
stipend near home can beat a bigger one in a city where you pay rent.

## Signs the page is from a previous cycle

Still common, because plenty of 2027 pages have not been updated yet:

- Dates in 2025 or 2026 with no mention of 2027
- "Applications are closed", "check back in the fall", "will open in November"
- A deadline that has already passed

None of these mean the program is gone. They mean the new cycle is not posted.
Note the date and let the weekly check catch the update. You do not have to run
anything: it runs every Monday on its own, and what it found sits at the top of
The Plan. A page that changes, and especially one that starts mentioning 2027,
is usually a program posting its new dates.

One thing it cannot do. Some sites refuse an automated reader, so the check
never sees them at all. CHOP CRISSP is one of them, and one to watch: its page
says it opens in December, and it runs a behavioral track alongside the basic
and clinical ones. The Plan lists every one under the note that says those
sites block the weekly check. Open those pages yourself every couple of weeks,
because nothing is going to tell you when they post.

## When the page says almost nothing

Some real programs have one thin paragraph and an email address. That is not a
bad sign. It often means placements are arranged directly rather than through a
portal, which is good news for you: more than a year of running participant
sessions and coding data is exactly what a short email can show.

In that case skip the application entirely and go straight to a
[cold email](../templates/cold-email-pi.md) to the listed contact or to a
relevant PI. UCSF pediatrics and the UC Davis MIND Institute work this way, and
so do three of the places you could commute to. Georgetown, George Washington
and Inova post a page about research and training rather than a summer
application with a deadline. GW's pediatrics department is based at Children's
National, so an email there can reach the same hospital by another route. And
Inova is in Fairfax, where you worked as a medical assistant last summer: if
anyone you met there knows someone at Inova, an introduction beats a cold email.
