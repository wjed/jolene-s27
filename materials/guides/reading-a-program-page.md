# Reading a program page in five minutes

Program pages bury the things that decide whether an application is worth
starting. This is the order to look for them.

---

## The five disqualifiers. Check these first

Before reading a single word about the research, find:

1. **Citizenship requirement.** "US citizen or permanent resident" appears on
   most federally funded programs. As a US citizen you clear this one, so look
   for residency rules instead: NYSCF takes only New York City residents, which
   is why it is no longer on the site.
2. **Class year.** Look for "rising senior", "must have completed sophomore
   year", "may not graduate before/during the program". Fred Hutch takes only
   students entering the summer before their final year, which is you. Seattle
   Children's turns away anyone graduating before or during the program.
3. **GPA minimum.** Often in a FAQ rather than the main page.
4. **Dates.** Fixed start dates (St. Jude has four and only four), and whether
   the full term is mandatory.
5. **Deadline, and what it covers.** Specifically: are letters due on the same
   date, or later? Penn SUIP's letters must arrive by the application deadline
   itself. The Broad gives letters one extra day, and Seattle Children's gives
   them fifteen. St. Jude's deadline covers every credential.

If any of these rule you out, stop. That is a win, four hours saved.

## Then find the mentor list

The most valuable page on any program site, and often two clicks deep:
"Faculty", "Mentors", "Research areas", "Labs", or a PDF of last year's projects.

This is where the [why-this-program paragraph](../templates/why-this-program.md)
and the [cold emails](../templates/cold-email-pi.md) come from. A program with no
published mentor list is one where naming a mentor matters less, and a program
with a long list is one where naming a mentor matters a great deal.

## Then the practicalities

- **Stipend**, and whether housing is separate. The two numbers only mean
  something together. A $7,500 stipend with room, board and travel included
  (Jackson Lab at Bar Harbor) is worth far more than $5,000 with no housing in
  Boston.
- **Housing**: provided, subsidized, or your problem. "Not provided" is only
  bad news if you have to pay rent, which is what the Leesburg rule below is
  about.
- **Travel**: Jackson Lab and several others reimburse it; most do not.
- **Length**: usually ten weeks, sometimes eight or nine (St. Jude wants eleven
  from undergraduates), and whether it collides with the end of your spring
  semester.

The dataset already has all of this, but verify it on the page before applying.
The stipend figures in particular are the fields most likely to be stale.

## The Leesburg rule

A program you can commute to from home is worth a smaller stipend, because
housing is free at home. With no rent to pay, a stipend with no housing is the
whole stipend. The same offer in Seattle, Boston or New York loses most of it to
rent.

Within reach of Leesburg, roughly, without traffic:

- **Janelia** in Ashburn, about 15 minutes. Its summer undergraduates come
  through HHMI's Cech Fellows Program, which recruits from partner schools, so
  check whether Penn State is one.
- **Inova** in Fairfax, about 35 minutes.
- **NIH** in Bethesda, about 45 minutes. NIH SIP provides no housing, which
  does not matter when you are driving in from home.
- **Children's National and Georgetown** in Washington, about an hour.
- **Johns Hopkins and Kennedy Krieger** in Baltimore, about 70 minutes. A long
  commute, but a real one, and Hopkins Pathway houses its interns for free
  anyway.

So when you compare offers, compare what you keep. A smaller stipend near home
can beat a bigger one in a city where you pay rent.

## Signs the page is from a previous cycle

Still common, because plenty of 2027 pages have not been updated yet:

- Dates in 2025 or 2026 with no mention of 2027
- "Applications are closed", "check back in the fall", "will open in November"
- A deadline that has already passed

None of these mean the program is gone. They mean the new cycle is not posted.
Note the date and let the weekly check catch the update. You do not have to run
anything: it runs every Monday on its own, and what it found sits at the top of
The Plan. A page that changes, and especially one that starts mentioning 2027,
is usually a program posting its new dates.

One thing it cannot do. A few sites refuse an automated reader, so the check
never sees them at all. CHOP CRISSP, which opens in December, is one of them.
The Plan lists every one under the note that says those sites block the weekly
check. Open those pages yourself every couple of weeks, because nothing is going
to tell you when they post.

## When the page says almost nothing

Some real programs have one thin paragraph and an email address. That is not a
bad sign. It often means placements are arranged directly rather than through a
portal, which is good news for anyone willing to write an email.

In that case skip the application entirely and go straight to a
[cold email](../templates/cold-email-pi.md) to the listed contact or to a
relevant PI. The NIA Alzheimer's centers, UCSF pediatrics and the UC Davis MIND
Institute all work this way, and so do two of the places you could commute to:
Georgetown and Inova post a page about research and training rather than a
summer application with a deadline.
