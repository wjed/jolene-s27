# Master personal statement

Write this **once**, properly. Then every application becomes: paste the master,
swap the program-specific paragraph, adjust length. That is the difference
between applying to six programs and applying to twenty.

Target length: 600-800 words. Most programs ask for 500-1000, and a tight 650
reads better than a padded 950.

Write it over Thanksgiving break. Seattle Children's, your earliest deadline,
closes January 4, 2027.

Your statement already has a spine. More than five years of shadowing a
pediatrician and over a year in the Depression and Anxiety Lab at Penn State
meet in one question: how mood and anxiety disorders show up in children.
Paragraphs 1 to 3 below follow that line.

---

## The shape

**Paragraph 1, why pediatrics (100-150 words)**

Not "I have always loved working with children." Your root is more than five
years of shadowing a pediatrician. Do not summarize the years: pick one visit,
the thing you noticed, and the question it left you with.

> Weak: "Shadowing a pediatrician for more than five years has shown me how
> much I love caring for children."
>
> Better: "[ONE VISIT FROM THOSE YEARS]. What stuck with me was not [the obvious
> part] but [the specific thing you noticed]. I wanted to know [the actual
> question]."

If a visit showed you how worry or low mood looks in a child, it leads straight
into paragraphs 2 and 3, but use it only if it happened. A personal reason
behind the pediatric interest goes here too, stated plainly, once, without
reaching for drama.

**Paragraph 2, what the lab has taught you (150-200 words)**

Lead with the Depression and Anxiety Lab: what it is asking (how mood disorders
show up in movement and affect), what *you* do there, and what that has taught
you about doing research, not just what the project is about.

Committees read for evidence that someone can be trusted with ten weeks of a
mentor's time: running something independently, handling a protocol, dealing
with something that did not work. You have all three. You run participant
sessions, you troubleshoot study protocols when they break, and you started
review sessions for a 300-student psychology course on your own. Tell one
instance properly.

> "In the Depression and Anxiety Lab at Penn State, I [WHAT YOU DO, in plain
> verbs: run participant sessions, code data, troubleshoot the protocol]. [ONE
> TIME A SESSION OR THE PROTOCOL DID NOT GO AS PLANNED, AND WHAT YOU DID]. That
> taught me [SOMETHING REAL ABOUT RESEARCH, not a platitude]."

Then a clause or a sentence each:

- **CITI training**: Students in Research, Social and Behavioral Human Subjects
  Research, and Good Clinical Practice. Most undergraduates have none of these,
  and they tell a mentor running human-subjects studies that you know the rules.
- **Clinical work**, for what it shows a mentor: up to 26 patients a day as a
  medical assistant says you are accurate at volume, and more than 100
  emergency calls as a volunteer EMT says you follow procedure under pressure.
  The rest stays on the [CV](../templates/cv-skeleton.md).
- **What you do not have**, said plainly: bench experience, posters,
  publications. Do not apologize or imply otherwise. For a bench lab, one
  sentence covers it: your research so far is with human subjects, you have
  taken organic chemistry and biochemistry, and [WHAT CARRIES OVER TO THE
  BENCH].

**Paragraph 3, what you want to learn next (120-180 words)**

The research area, and why. Be specific enough that a reader could guess which
labs to route the application to. Yours is where pediatrics and developmental
psychology meet: how mood and anxiety disorders show up in children. "Pediatric
research" tells a reader nothing; "how anxiety shows up in children's behavior,
and how early it can be seen" tells them where to send the file.

The bridge from paragraph 2 is already built. Your lab studies how mood
disorders show up in movement and affect, so ask what that looks like in a
child: [YOUR QUESTION, in your words]. Then say what the summer would teach you
that your lab cannot: [WHAT IT WOULD ADD, such as working with children and
families].

Naming a method helps: yours (participant sessions, data coding,
technology-based emotion tracking), and the lab's, such as EEG or
patient-registry data, once you have read its papers.

Keep mood and anxiety in children at the center and tilt it per program, toward
neuroscience or pediatric neurology for a brain sciences program and toward
clinical research for a clinical one. Tilting is fine; a new interest for every
program is not.

**Paragraph 4, why this program, specifically (100-150 words)**

The one paragraph that changes every time. See
[the why-this-program guide](../templates/why-this-program.md). Never send an
application where this paragraph could have been addressed to any other
institution. The **For you** line on each program is a fair place to start, but
the paragraph still needs a lab, a person or a paper from that program.

**Paragraph 5, where this goes (80-120 words)**

You are headed for medicine and have not decided between an MD and an MD-PhD.
Undecided is a fine answer. What is not fine is vagueness that reads as not
having thought about it, so say what the summer would help you decide.

> "I plan to become a physician, and I have not yet decided whether that means
> an MD or an MD-PhD. This summer would tell me [WHAT YOU WANT TO FIND OUT]."

Amgen Scholars, the Broad, Penn SUIP, the Leadership Alliance, MIT MSRP and
WashU's BioMedRAP are PhD-pipeline programs. The first four also count an
MD-PhD, so they are open to you if that question is real; say so honestly
rather than writing yourself as PhD-bound. Penn State SURIP says it is not
meant for students who plan to apply to medical school, which you will do
either way. If you land on MD-only, those hours belong to the hospital-based
programs, and the statement should not pretend otherwise.

## Three that need a different shape

Most applications take the master with paragraph 4 swapped. These do not:

- **The Broad** wants five short essays, not one statement (closes
  January 10, 2027). Build them from your paragraphs.
- **UPMC Children's Hospital of Pittsburgh** asks for nothing but a statement
  of purpose (closes January 11, 2027), so it carries your lab work, CITI
  training and clinical hours on its own.
- **Yale Child Study Center** wants a one-page letter of interest (closes
  January 14, 2027). It is a developmental science internship, so keep
  paragraphs 3 and 4 nearly whole and cut the rest to a sentence or two each.

## Before sending, check

- [ ] Could another premed with shadowing hours have written this paragraph?
      Rewrite it.
- [ ] Does the program name appear, spelled correctly, in paragraph 4?
- [ ] Is there at least one specific paper, lab or faculty member named?
- [ ] Any sentence about loving science or children? Replace it with something
      you saw or did.
- [ ] Could anyone identify a patient or participant you mention? Cut the
      detail.
- [ ] Does anything imply bench work, a poster or a publication? Fix it.
- [ ] Read it aloud. Anywhere you stumble, a reader will too.
- [ ] Within the word limit. Over-length gets cut or counted against.
- [ ] Someone else proofread it. Not spellcheck, a person.

## Three openings that work

1. **The question.** Open with what you want to know about mood and anxiety in
   children, then how you came to care about it.
2. **The moment.** One visit from your years of shadowing, and the question it
   raised. An EMT call or a patient from your medical assistant summer can work
   too, but only if it leads to your research question, not just to medicine.
3. **The problem.** Something unresolved about how mood disorders show up in
   children, and where you want to stand in relation to it. Strongest if you
   have read the papers behind your lab's work; risky if you have not.

Avoid: quotations from famous scientists, dictionary definitions, "Webster's
defines", and childhood-illness framing unless it is genuinely yours to tell.
