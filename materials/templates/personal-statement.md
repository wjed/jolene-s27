# Master personal statement

Write this **once**, properly. Then every application becomes: paste the master,
swap the program-specific paragraph, adjust length. That is the difference
between applying to six programs and applying to twenty.

Target length: 600-800 words. Most programs ask for 500-1000, and a tight 650
reads better than a padded 950.

---

## The shape

**Paragraph 1, the specific thing that got you here (100-150 words)**

Not "I have always loved science." One concrete moment, experience or question,
and what it made you want to find out. If there is a personal reason behind the
pediatric interest, this is where it goes, stated plainly, once, without
reaching for drama.

> Weak: "Ever since I was young, I have been fascinated by the human brain."
>
> Better: "[CONCRETE MOMENT]. What stuck with me was not [the obvious part] but
> [the specific thing you noticed]. I wanted to know [the actual question]."

**Paragraph 2, what you have actually done (150-200 words)**

Research experience, coursework that genuinely matters, lab skills, clinical or
volunteer work. For each thing: what the question was, what *you* did, and what
you learned about doing research, not just what the project was about.

Committees read for evidence that someone can be trusted with ten weeks of a
mentor's time. Signals that help: you ran something independently, you handled a
protocol, you dealt with a result that did not work.

> "In [COURSE/LAB], I [SPECIFIC TASK, the assay, the dataset, the protocol].
> [WHAT WENT WRONG OR SURPRISED YOU]. That taught me [SOMETHING REAL ABOUT
> RESEARCH, not a platitude]."

If you have little or no formal research experience: say so without apology and
lean on transferable evidence, a hard course with a project component, a data
analysis you did, a job that required precision and follow-through. Several
programs on this list (Seattle Children's especially) explicitly prioritise
students *without* prior research experience.

**Paragraph 3, what you want to work on (120-180 words)**

The research area, and why. Be specific enough that a reader could guess which
labs to route the application to. "Pediatric neurology, particularly how seizure
burden affects development" is useful. "Biomedical research" is not.

Naming a method or model system helps: EEG, mouse models, patient-registry data,
imaging, single-cell work.

**Paragraph 4, why this program, specifically (100-150 words)**

The one paragraph that changes every time.
See [why-this-program.md](why-this-program.md). Never send an application where
this paragraph could have been addressed to any other institution.

**Paragraph 5, where this goes (80-120 words)**

MD, PhD, MD-PhD, or honestly undecided between them, all fine. What is not fine
is vagueness that reads as not having thought about it. If undecided, say what
the summer would help you decide.

Note: Amgen Scholars, SUIP, MSRP and BioMedRAP are explicitly PhD-pipeline
programs. If you are aiming MD-only, those are a weaker fit than the
hospital-based programs, and the statement should not pretend otherwise.

## Before sending, check

- [ ] Could this paragraph have been written by any other applicant? Rewrite it.
- [ ] Does the program name appear, spelled correctly, in paragraph 4?
- [ ] Is there at least one specific paper, lab or faculty member named?
- [ ] Any sentence beginning "I am passionate about"? Delete it.
- [ ] Read aloud, anywhere you stumble, a reader will too.
- [ ] Within the word limit. Over-length gets cut or counted against.
- [ ] Someone else proofread it. Not spellcheck, a person.

## Three openings that work

1. **The question.** Open with the thing you genuinely want to know, then how
   you came to care about it.
2. **The moment.** A specific scene, in a lab, a clinic, a classroom, and the
   question it raised. Earns its place only if it leads somewhere.
3. **The problem.** Something unresolved in the field, and where you want to
   stand in relation to it. Strongest option if you have read real literature;
   risky if you have not.

Avoid: quotations from famous scientists, dictionary definitions, "webster's
defines", and childhood-illness framing unless it is genuinely yours to tell.
