# Your research CV

One page, and you have more than enough to fill it: a lab, five certifications,
three clinical roles, more than five years of shadowing and two teaching roles.
The work is in the order and the bullets, not in finding things to say. Your
letter requests and your emails to labs both attach this CV, so finish it
before you send either.

A research CV is not a job resume. The resume you have now was built for
clinical jobs: the lab is one line near the bottom and the CITI training is not
on it at all. This version turns that around, with education first, then the
lab in full, then the certificates, then the clinical work and the teaching.
Leave off any objective statement, and any line that just says
"Microsoft Office".

Wherever the CV below says `[TAB]`, the part after it sits flush against the
right margin. How to do that in Google Docs or Word, without spaces, is the
first rule under the CV.

## Rules that matter

- **Do not fake the right-hand column with spaces.** The CV marks it with
  [TAB] because spaces do not survive the trip into a word processor: in Google
  Docs or Word every run of spaces lands somewhere different and the right edge
  comes out ragged. Use a right-aligned tab stop. In Google Docs, click the
  ruler at the right margin and add a right tab stop there. In Word, open the
  Paragraph settings, choose Tabs, and set a right-aligned tab at the right
  margin. Then press Tab once wherever the CV says [TAB]. A two-column table
  with the borders turned off works too. Both survive editing and the PDF
  export.
- **Use the bullet button** for the · lines instead of typing the dots, so a
  bullet that wraps onto a second line stays indented.
- **Reverse chronological** within every section, with the roles you still
  hold first.
- **Present tense for what you still do, past tense for what you have
  finished.** "Run participant sessions" for the lab, "Took vital signs" for
  the summer job.
- **No pronouns.** "Code study data," not "I code study data."
- **Verbs first.** Not "Responsible for troubleshooting study protocols" but
  "Troubleshoot study protocols."
- **Be specific about skills.** "Experience with research participants" says
  little. "Participant sessions, data coding, protocol troubleshooting" says
  more, and the name of the software or coding scheme says more still. List
  only what you could do unsupervised tomorrow.
- **Quantify where it is honest.** You already have numbers most applicants do
  not: more than 100 emergency calls, up to 26 patients a day, 80 students as
  a Learning Assistant and a 300-student course as a TA. The lab entry needs
  its own, such as sessions run and hours a week.
- **Certifications near the top.** Most undergraduate applicants have no CITI
  training, and you have three certificates, Good Clinical Practice among
  them. Here they sit right under your research, where a human-subjects lab
  sees them first. Copy each course title exactly as it reads on the
  certificate. If you add the biomedical CITI course this winter, it goes with
  them.
- **Coursework: the four to six courses that matter most.** Biochemistry and
  organic chemistry lead, then your psychology courses by name and any
  statistics or research methods. General chemistry, physics and calculus are
  on every premed transcript, so they go last, and they come off first if the
  list runs past two lines.
- **One page, 10-11pt, generous margins.** A cramped two-pager reads worse than
  a clean one-pager. If yours runs over, cut the lifeguard line first, then
  shorten the clinical bullets, and leave the lab alone. Yale Child Study
  Center asks for a two-page resume, so for that one application you can put
  the cut detail back.
- **Home on the contact line.** For the city, use home in Loudoun County
  rather than State College. For a program or lab near home, that tells the
  reader you would commute and need no housing: the
  [living-at-home rule](../guides/reading-a-program-page.md) at work.
- **Export to PDF**, named `Lastname-Firstname-CV.pdf`. Never send a .docx.

## One lab, no bench work, no posters yet

That is a normal research section for the summer before senior year, and plenty
of programs on the site take students with no bench experience at all. Seattle
Children's says prior research experience is not required and prioritizes
first-time research interns. For developmental and clinical research, where
your interests point, participant sessions and CITI training are the right
experience, not a stand-in for bench work.

- **Write the lab entry in full.** It is the entry a research program reads
  most closely, so give it every bullet it can honestly carry. More than a year
  in one lab is the follow-through a reviewer looks for.
- **A real course project counts too.** If a course had you ask a question and
  collect or analyze data to answer it, give it its own entry under the lab:
  the question, and what you did. A lab course that followed set procedures is
  coursework, not research.
- **Leave bench techniques off.** You have not done bench work, and anything on
  the page is fair game in an interview. Do not dress the human-subjects work
  up in bench language either. For a bench program, let the coursework line
  speak instead: organic chemistry and biochemistry show you have the
  background. If a program wants bench experience, read its fit rating and the
  For you note on the site before you spend the hours.
- **No presentations or publications section yet.** Add one the day a poster
  or abstract is accepted, and not before: an empty or padded section reads
  worse than none. A poster at a campus symposium counts. List the authors in
  the order on the abstract, then the title, where and when it was presented,
  and whether it was a poster or a talk.
- **Leave high-school material off.** The shadowing is the exception: more than
  five years with one pediatrician earns its line.
