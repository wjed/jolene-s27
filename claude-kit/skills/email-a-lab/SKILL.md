# Emailing a lab

This is the highest-return thing in the whole application process and almost
nobody does it. Many summer programs let applicants name a preferred mentor,
and plenty of placements come down to a faculty member saying "I would take
this student". Some positions, like the Boston Children's epilepsy lab or the
NIA Alzheimer's centres, are only reachable this way.

Fifteen minutes per email. Three or four per program she seriously wants.

## Finding the right person

1. Open the program page and find the faculty or mentor list. It is usually two
   clicks deep, under "Faculty", "Mentors", "Research areas" or "Labs".
2. Shortlist two or three whose work connects to developmental psychology,
   pediatrics, mood and anxiety disorders, or behavioural measurement. Those are
   her actual interests; do not shortlist on prestige.
3. Look up one recent paper each on PubMed. Read the abstract properly.
4. Check the lab is active. If the most recent paper is from 2019, it may be
   winding down. Say so rather than drafting the email.
5. Take the address from the faculty page, not from a paper's corresponding
   author line, which is often stale.

If there is no published mentor list, say so. That usually means placements are
arranged centrally, and the email matters less.

## The draft

Under 200 words. They are reading it on a phone between meetings.

> **Subject:** Summer 2027 research, undergraduate interested in [THEIR TOPIC]
>
> Dear Dr. [LAST NAME],
>
> I am a third-year Integrative Sciences major at Penn State applying to
> [PROGRAM NAME] for summer 2027. I am writing because I would like to work in
> your lab.
>
> I read your [YEAR] paper on [SPECIFIC FINDING, not the title]. [ONE SENTENCE
> THAT SHOWS SHE READ IT: what surprised her, a question it raised, or how it
> connects to what she wants to understand.]
>
> Since August 2025 I have worked in a Depression and Anxiety Lab at Penn State,
> running participant sessions, coding data, and working on technology-based
> emotion tracking. I have completed CITI human subjects and GCP training. I am
> also a volunteer EMT with Centre LifeLink, so I am comfortable around patients.
>
> Are you taking a summer student in 2027? If the application asks for a
> preferred mentor, I would like to name you, but only if that would be welcome.
>
> My CV is attached. Thank you for your time.
>
> Jolene VanderHeyden
> Penn State, Class of 2028

## Rules

- **One specific sentence about their actual work.** Generic praise is worse
  than none. If you cannot write that sentence, you have not read the paper.
- **Ask for one thing**: whether they are taking a summer student. Not "please
  mentor me".
- **Attach the CV** as one PDF named `VanderHeyden-Jolene-CV.pdf`.
- **No follow-up for ten days.** Then exactly one, and never a third.
- **Draft, do not send.** Show her the email and let her send it. Never send on
  her behalf.

## What replies mean

- *"Yes, name me"*: excellent. Put it in the application immediately and say so
  explicitly, "Dr. X has agreed to host me". That line carries real weight.
- *"I am not taking students, but talk to Dr. Y"*: a warm introduction. Use it.
- *"Apply through the central program"*: neutral, but she is now a name they
  have seen rather than one of four hundred files.
- Silence: the normal outcome. Not personal. Ten emails producing one yes is a
  good rate, and that one yes can decide the summer.
